Freeing the team to free you

What three team interviews say about where work waits, and what we'd build to fix it.

Prepared for Ian by Alexandra. Based on 3 team interviews (October 21, 23 and 26), the written follow-ups, the August team survey and the ClickUp audit of October 5.

Internal working document. Not a client deliverable.

"The video production, all that stuff runs really smoothly […] there's really no hiccup on that end of stuff." (the team)
  • Situation: the team produces fast once it has what it needs.
  • Complication: footage, client information, headlines and decisions all go through you, and that's where work waits for days or weeks.
  • What we'd do: make four handoffs happen at a fixed moment, without you having to remember them, and stop the team typing the same thing three times.
None of this needs more people or more of your time. It needs information to leave your head and your computer on its own.

05 / 19
Root cause

The information lives with you, and nothing makes it move at a set time

Footage sits on your computer. Client context sits in your calls. Headline taste sits in your head. None of it has a fixed moment to reach the team, so the team asks, follows up and waits. Every question adds to your load, which slows the next answer.

You're overloaded
→
Information stays with you
→
Team asks and follows up
→
More pings to you
"The problem is I'm doing everything all at once." (Ian, October 21)
The fix isn't asking you to do more. It's making the handoff happen without you having to remember it.

Build 1 · Handoff after the sale

1. A kickoff brief drafted from your sales call

The problem. The team learns about new clients second-hand, then asks you on Slack. Names, accesses and deadlines arrive late.

How it works

Your call with the client (Zoom)
→
Summary and transcript
→
AI drafts the kickoff brief
→
Team fills the gaps
→
You confirm in Slack
→
Project created in ClickUp
  • The brief uses the 2025 Client Project Onboarding sheet the team already had, plus the PandaDoc deliverables.
  • Fields: client and contacts, who gives final approval on the client side, deliverables, people to film with exact names, accesses to request (YouTube, Google Ads, sometimes LinkedIn), assets, deadlines, publishing pace, preferences.
  • The brief lands in the client's Slack channel and in the client notes doc in ClickUp.
  • Anything the AI couldn't find becomes a question to you in the same Slack thread. You answer once.

What you do

  • Have the call as usual
  • Answer the open questions in one thread
  • Click confirm

What the team does

  • Check the draft
  • Add accesses and assets
  • Create the project from the brief

To check first

  • Whether Zoom's summary can be pulled automatically (its API is reported unreliable)
  • if not, a note-taker such as Fathom on your Zoom calls
Every new client starts with a complete brief in the first days, without a kickoff meeting.

Agent comfort medium (Zoom access is the unknown). Human checking low once the brief template is agreed.

Build 2 · Footage (2/2)

2. Footage: a shoot log the AI writes on its own

The problem. Nobody knows what was filmed. Names get googled, intros go missing, a podcast camera has been missing for three or four months, and sometimes a video you expect doesn't exist.

How it works

  • Who was there. The AI reads the shoot in your calendar and the kickoff brief (build 1): client, date, people to film with exact names, plus the client's contacts in your email.
  • Which clip is who. Every clip carries the time it was filmed. The AI matches clips to the calendar slot, then reads the Descript transcript, where people introduce themselves or get named.
  • The log. One line per take: name, company, topic, length and timecode. A name it can't confirm is flagged with a still from the clip, never guessed.
  • Check. The log posts in the client's Slack channel. The team confirms the flagged names.

What you do

  • Keep shoots in your calendar with the client's name
  • Nothing during the shoot

What the team does

  • Confirm flagged names
  • Use the log for labels and missing footage

To check first

  • Camera clocks on the right time
  • Shoots are in your calendar
  • How often guests say their name on camera
Correct names and a complete inventory, without anyone having to remember a thing on set.

Agent comfort medium (calendar, clip times and transcript to match). Human checking low: the team confirms flagged names.

Build 3 · Headlines

3. A headline assistant trained on your edits

The problem. 15-30 headlines a week go through you. You change them often and return them in batches. One video sat for months.

How it works

  • Collect. Your before/after edits already sit in each client's Airtable (the "For Ian (Headlines)" view and the notes). We export them. Today the team has no headlines tab it can add to, so it built its own table: that list moves into the same place.
  • Teach. The assistant gets your past edits as examples, plus the rules the team already knows: keywords, the client's or company's name, 100 characters max, capitalization.
  • Suggest. For each new video it proposes a headline and says how sure it is.
  • You see only the exceptions. Confident headlines go ahead. Uncertain ones come to you in one weekly batch.
  • Test. Before switching, it runs in the background on the next batch and we compare its headlines with yours.
  • Reuse. We check what your YouTube Optimizer already does before building anything new.

What you do

  • Nothing new at first
  • Then review flagged headlines only

What the team does

  • Pick from the suggestions
  • Stop copying headlines between tools (build 4)

Bonus

  • YouTube's Test & Compare lets a channel test up to 3 titles or thumbnails and keeps the one with the most watch time, if the client gives the team access
Videos stop waiting on headlines. You see a handful of exceptions, not every title.

Agent comfort high. Human checking medium: you judge whether its headlines are good enough.

Build 4 · Tools

4. One entry, everywhere

The problem. One editor assignment today means: Slack message → ClickUp comment → editor's sheet → editor's Slack channel. Statuses are copied into the editor's sheet, ClickUp and the client's Airtable.

How it works

ClickUp becomes the one place where things are entered; Slack stays the place to talk. A small script listens to ClickUp and does the copying.

Assign a task in ClickUp
→
Script hears it
→
Posts in #mark_video or #jayson_video
→
Adds the row to the editor's sheet
Change a status in ClickUp
→
Script hears it
→
Updates the client's Airtable
  • Vimeo comments. The script checks Vimeo for new client comments and posts them on the right ClickUp task and in Slack.
  • Headlines. Typed once in Airtable; the ClickUp task follows.
  • Where it runs. Small scripts on our own server, no Zapier. ClickUp and Airtable both have the hooks needed.

What you do

  • Nothing

What the team does

  • Enter things once, in ClickUp

To check first

  • Whether Mark and Jayson read ClickUp (then their sheets can go)
  • whether Vimeo sends comment alerts or we check every few minutes
  • which Zaps the team already runs (they aren't documented)
  • Descript stays manual
One entry per change. The team feels it first, and it needs nothing from you.

Agent comfort high. Human checking low: the team confirms on a few real tasks.

Build 5 · Decisions

5. One decision hour a week

The problem. The team follows up daily and answers come at the end of the week. Question sets, approved videos and priorities wait.

How it works

  • A "Waiting on Ian" status in ClickUp. Anything that needs you gets it.
  • Before the hour, a script posts one list in Slack: tasks waiting on you, headlines flagged by the assistant, videos ready to publish, questions from new briefs.
  • One fixed hour with Danika. You go down the list. Decisions are written back on the tasks, so a decision you give one person by DM reaches the whole team.
  • Anything urgent still goes to Slack, as today.

What you do

  • Keep one fixed hour
  • Go down the list

What the team does

  • Tag what waits on you
  • Stop chasing during the week
A week of follow-ups becomes one hour. The team knows your priorities.

Agent comfort high. Human checking low. Depends on a clean ClickUp (build 7).

Build 6 · Production

6. Three production speed-ups

Topic index per interview

  • AI reads each interview's Descript transcript and lists the topics with timecodes
  • Stored next to the interview in Airtable
  • Finding a passage for a revision takes minutes instead of at least an hour
  • Answers "do we have content on X?" across all interviews

Thumbnails in about 10 minutes

  • One Canva brand template per client, made once at onboarding
  • AI image generation (OpenAI's image model) builds the background from a real video frame and the client's style
  • Text is added in Canva, because AI still struggles with exact text
  • Target about 10 minutes, one hour at most, instead of about a day

Captions that stay in sync

  • Captions drift after about 40 seconds on YouTube
  • Drift that grows over time usually means a frame-rate mismatch between the video and the caption file
  • We test on a short clip, fix the export setting once, and the 15-20 minutes per video disappear
Three daily tasks shrink from hours to minutes.

Agent comfort high for all three. Human checking low, except thumbnails: Danika approves the template once per client.

ClickUp. "Closed" counts as open today, so about 1,270 parent tasks look active. Builds 4 and 5 would send noise until that's fixed.

  • Make "Closed" a real closed status.
  • Merge Case Story clients into Delivery.
  • Keep the production statuses and the automatic reassignment the team relies on.

Client record. One place per client: contacts, deliverables, preferences, branding, Slack channel, and accesses.

  • The ClickUp client notes doc already exists; the kickoff brief (build 1) fills it automatically.

Build 2 · Full guide

Same-day footage, step by step

What changes for you. Nothing during the shoot. At the end of the day, the files go into one shared folder instead of your computer. The color pass moves to the team.

Option A: no new gear (we start here)

Before the shoot

  • A script reads the shoot in your calendar and creates an empty Google Drive folder for it, named client + date, shared with the team.
  • The laptop that goes to the shoot has Google Drive for desktop installed, so anything dropped in that folder uploads on its own.
  • Both camera clocks are set to the right time zone. The shoot log (next slide) relies on the time each clip was filmed.
  • Pocket 4K: format the USB-C SSD and record ProRes rather than Blackmagic RAW, so Descript can open the files. We test one clip before the first real shoot.

During the shoot

  • Pocket 4K records straight to the USB-C SSD. Nikon Z5 records to its card as usual.
  • Nothing to say or note. The log is built afterwards.

At the end of the day (about 10 minutes of hands-on time)

  • You, or a local helper if you're travelling, plug the SSD and the Z5 card into the laptop.
  • Drag the files into the shoot folder. Leave the laptop on and connected overnight.
  • Upload time depends on the connection. A shoot of about 500 GB takes roughly 11 hours at 100 Mbps upload, or about 2 hours at 500 Mbps. That's why it runs overnight.
  • A message posts in the client's Slack channel when the folder stops growing: "Footage for [client] is in Drive."

Next morning (the team)

  • Import from Google Drive into Descript.
  • Apply one fixed color look (LUT) per camera, made once for the Z5 and once for the Pocket 4K, then Descript's built-in adjustments if needed. No color pass on your side.
  • Set every deadline for the client that same day.

Option B: camera to cloud (only if A still slips)

  • Neither camera uploads on its own: Blackmagic Cloud upload is for the 6K models, and Frame.io's Nikon support covers the Z6III, Z8, Z9 and ZR, not the Z5.
  • So we add a recorder on the HDMI out of the main interview camera. Recommended: Atomos Ninja TX GO, $799 list price (seen around $560 on sale in July 2026). It has Wi-Fi and Frame.io Camera to Cloud built in, so no extra module.
  • It records the full-quality file on its own card (CFexpress Type B, bought separately) and sends a small preview copy to Frame.io while you shoot.
  • On set it needs Wi-Fi or a phone hotspot.
  • The team watches and picks takes from the preview copies the same day. The full files still arrive through the Option A folder, so edits start a day earlier, not a week earlier.
  • Frame.io Pro is $15 per member per month.
  • Older routes cost more for the same result: Ninja V with the Atomos Connect module, or the Shogun Connect ($1,299 at launch). The Ninja TX ($999 list) only adds SDI and wireless timecode, which you don't need.

Option C: a phone as second angle

  • The free Blackmagic Camera app records on a phone and uploads to Blackmagic Cloud on its own.
  • Good for behind-the-scenes or a second angle, not for the main interview.

How we know it works

  • We note the number of days between shoot and footage in Descript, starting with the next shoot. Target: one day.

Still to check

  • Whether Descript opens Pocket 4K ProRes files directly, and how big a ProRes LT shoot is compared with today.
  • The upload speed where you usually finish a shoot.
  • Whether the Ninja TX GO needs a paid Atomos plan for Frame.io uploads.
